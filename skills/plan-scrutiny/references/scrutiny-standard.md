# The scrutiny standard

This list was distilled from a leadership team reviewing an operating plan over nineteen revisions. Each item is something a reviewer actually sent back, generalised so it applies to any plan. The reason is given because the reason is what lets you apply the check to a situation the list does not cover.

Use it twice: as a drafting checklist, and as the category list for the rehearsal.

## A. Language

**A1. Plain words, no punch lines.** Reviewers sent back "the system in seven parts", "learn and adapt, every week" and similar lines as "cliché, not needed". A heading should say what the section is. A sentence should say what happens. If a line would work in an advert, cut it. Why: senior reviewers read punch lines as a sign the author is selling rather than explaining, and they stop trusting the detail.

**A2. Every term must survive the question "what does that mean?"** "Register kept by hand", "the account is a person", "measurement is hard to source" were all sent back. Why: the reviewer who does not understand a term cannot evaluate the step, so they reject the step.

**A3. Call weak points gaps, not problems.** Why: "problem" reads as blame and invites a defence; "gap" reads as a distance to close and invites a plan.

**A4. Common terminology.** Use the names the organisation uses (Teams, not Slack; the real tool, not "the outreach tool"). Why: a wrong tool name tells the reviewer the author does not know the operation.

## B. Reality

**B1. Real names, real accounts, real tools.** No "Campaign A, B, C", no "the ISR", no placeholder people when real ones exist. Why: reviewers check the plan against the people they manage. Placeholders cannot be checked, so they are not believed.

**B2. Say what exists today and what its limit is.** A plan marked several steps as gaps that the current tool already did in a weaker form. The reviewer's reply: "these are present today, clarify it better." Why: overstating a gap is the fastest way to lose a reviewer who knows the current state.

**B3. Do not invent profiles, data or results.** If a number is an assumption, label it. If a person's role is unconfirmed, mark it "to confirm". Why: one invented fact discovered in the room invalidates the rest.

## C. Completeness of each step

**C1. For every step: what happens, who does it, what comes in, from where, provided by whom.** The reviewer clicked a step called "Bring in contacts" and asked exactly that. Why: a step without inputs and providers cannot be run by anyone but the author.

**C2. Every branch of every decision leads somewhere.** When a toggle was set to "no ISR available", the flow simply stopped. The reviewer: "no, the Sales Activation owners look into the reply and hand it over, and we need to be detailed on what handover means." Why: the unhappy branch is where real work happens, and it is the branch reviewers test first.

**C3. Cold, failure and leaver paths exist.** What happens when the prospect goes quiet, when the tool throws a warning, when the person running the account leaves. Why: these are the business-continuity questions, and a plan that only covers the happy path is a demo, not a plan.

**C4. Detail is nested, not flattened.** The initial view shows the flow, the logic and the people; the nuance opens when a step is clicked or expanded. Reviewers asked for sections to be "minimised with an option to expand" three separate times. Why: leadership was irritated by a flow that "looked complicated". Density on the first screen is read as confusion.

## D. Ownership

**D1. Every block has an owner by name and a backup.** "Who holds responsibility should also be added." Why: a plan with no owner is a wish.

**D2. Roles and responsibilities are written down per person.** What they own, what they do in the flow, who backs them up. Why: the same reviewer who asks "who owns this" will next ask "and what exactly do they do".

**D3. Missing stakeholders get added, not assumed.** The client-side feedback collectors were absent until a reviewer named them. Ask early: who else touches this, who gives feedback, who approves changes. Why: a stakeholder discovered in the room becomes a redesign.

## E. Control

**E1. A person approves anything that leaves the company or cannot be undone.** "No handover email goes out on its own; it should always be sent to the rep to approve and send manually. Confirm everywhere." Why: an automated external action is the first thing a reviewer imagines going wrong, and the plan must already have the answer.

**E2. One variable, one meaning, every section.** When the flow had an ISR/no-ISR switch, the reviewer required the replies section to follow the same switch. Why: inconsistency between sections suggests the sections were written separately and not thought through together.

**E3. Order matches how it is read.** "The flow goes bottom to top; let's go top down and ensure the same messaging is seen across." Why: reviewers read in one direction; a diagram that reads the other way costs them effort and goodwill.

## F. Reasoning

**F1. Every recommendation carries its logic.** The reviewer asked for job titles "with a logic section" rather than a list. Why: a list can be argued with line by line; a logic can be agreed once and then applied.

**F2. Every decision has a stated alternative that was rejected.** Why: "did you consider X" is the most common reviewer question, and "yes, and here is why not" ends it.

**F3. Every decision has a condition that would change it.** Why: it shows the decision is reasoned rather than fixed, and it tells the reviewer what evidence would move you.

## G. Improvement

**G1. There is a loop that reviews results and changes the plan on a schedule.** The reviewer asked for a continuous improvement layer "so we learn and adapt and show better insights", as a named capability with its own owner. Why: a plan without a review cadence is assumed to decay.

**G2. Feedback has a route into the plan.** Who collects it, where it is logged, who turns it into a change, who confirms the change back. Why: feedback with no route is lost, and the people giving it stop.

## H. Presentation of the deliverable

The plan is also a page, a deck or a document, and reviewers pick at that before they reach the logic. These items came from one review sitting on a finished deck: nine comments, none about the plan, all about whether it could be read.

**H1. It can be read from where the reviewer sits.** Secondary text inside cards is as bright as the heading's supporting line, never a muted grey on a dark card; nothing under 24px on a slide; no cramped card with four lines where the row has room for one. Why: a reviewer who cannot read the second line of a card assumes it hides something.

**H2. The space is used.** Half-empty slide with cramped text in one corner is sent back. Lists become titled rows; nine cards in one row become three by three. Why: cramped text next to empty space reads as a draft.

**H3. One term, everywhere.** The stage names, role names and tool names on slide 3 match slide 11 and match the plan behind the deck. "Was, is, becomes" on one slide and "Today, new way" on another is sent back as "use a better term". Why: a term that changes between sections makes the reviewer re-learn the plan.

**H4. Names are filled in or marked.** Every owner cell holds a real name or "to confirm, [who] by [when]". Why: a reviewer who knows the team fills the blank from memory and then distrusts the rest.

**H5. One message per unit.** A slide title says one thing in a full sentence; a card has a title and one supporting line; a table has at most the rows a reader can take in at once. Why: reviewers read the titles first and decide from them whether to read the rest.

**H6. Nothing is left open in the reviewer-facing version.** Every choice shows one answer and one owner. Open items go in a decisions list as questions, each with a recommendation and an owner, not scattered through the body. Why: a plan that asks the room to decide its contents is read as unfinished; a plan that recommends and asks for approval is read as a plan.

## Using the standard in a rehearsal

Pick two or three questions from each group A to H that fit the plan, and make sure every relevant perspective in `perspectives.md` has asked at least two of them. Ask them in the reviewer's voice, short and slightly impatient. The user answers without the document. Grade: clear, partial, cannot explain. Only "clear" passes.
